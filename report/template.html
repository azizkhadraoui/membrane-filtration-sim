Automating membrane filtration: what a simulated Franka cell shows

A food-microbiology lab filters up to 100 sugar-solution samples a day by hand. This study builds the whole per-sample sequence in MuJoCo with a Franka Panda, puts the hardest step (lifting a wet 47 mm membrane off the frit and laying it on agar without folds or air pockets) under randomised testing, trains a vision policy for it, and tests perception on the lab's own footage.

What this proves, and what it does not

Shown in simulation
  • An arm-based cell layout reaches every station: input/output plate stacks, membrane magazine, 6-position filter block, vessel rack, wash station.
  • The full per-sample sequence runs end to end, with measured step times.
  • Cycle time against the 5-minute target, including slow or stalled filtrations.
  • A vision policy can perform the membrane transfer on randomised layouts it has not seen.
  • Perception trained only on synthetic images, run on the lab's real footage.
Not shown
  • That a wet MCE membrane behaves like the simulated sheet: adhesion, surface tension and air entrapment on agar are not modelled.
  • Real tweezer or fingertip performance. The grasp is modelled as a clamp on the membrane edge.
  • Sim-to-real transfer of the policy.
  • Reliability under real lab lighting and occlusion, and production readiness.

The membrane is a stable thin-shell approximation tuned for solver stability, not for wet-membrane physics. The physics gap is exactly what the Franka phase is for.

The critical step

Membrane from frit to agar

The sheet is gripped at its edge, lifted so it hangs, and laid down far edge first. The tips then move forward and down while their tilt flattens out, so the contact line rolls across the membrane the way a technician avoids air pockets. Each run randomises membrane position (±3 mm) and rotation, plate position (±5 mm) and grasp direction.

Learned policy

An ACT policy (action-chunking transformer with a CVAE, implemented in PyTorch for this study) trained on {{N_DEMOS}} expert demonstrations with randomised lighting, colours and camera pose. During recording the expert's motion was pushed off course by small random offsets while the clean corrective target was recorded, so the policy also learns how to recover. Inputs are a top camera, a wrist camera and a close plate camera at 128×128 px, plus joint state. It outputs the tip pose and gripper command at 10 Hz, predicting 2-second chunks with temporal ensembling. Trained for {{STEPS}} steps (batch 48) on one RTX 4060 laptop GPU; held-out validation L1 on normalised actions {{VAL_L1}}.

LeRobot was not used: its current release pins an older torch that would have replaced the CUDA build on this machine. The dataset format is plain NumPy, so converting to a LeRobotDataset is a small step.

Throughput

Cycle time against the 5-minute target

A discrete-event model (SimPy) uses the step times measured in the sim cell. The arm is busy for loading and unloading. During filtration it is free to serve other positions; funnel rinsing and disinfection happen at the wash station, off the arm.

With one arm the cell levels off at about {{CYCLE}} minutes per sample once two positions run in parallel: the arm, not filtration, is the bottleneck. That is about 5.3 hours for 100 samples. If filtrations slow toward 10 minutes, four positions recover the same rate. A second arm, or faster arm motions, roughly halves it. Arm motions in the sim are deliberately conservative (0.25–0.35 m/s), so these step times are an upper bound.

Perception

Sim-trained perception on the lab's own footage

A segmentation network (U-Net, ResNet18 encoder) trained only on 5,235 rendered images with automatic labels for membrane, frit, funnel, dish, lid and agar. On held-out sim scenes it reaches mIoU {{SEG_MIOU}}.

On the lab's own videos it does not transfer yet. Of {{REAL_N}} points hand-placed on real frames before predictions were viewed, only {{REAL_FG}} of the object points are labelled correctly; real funnels mostly read as background and agar plates are not found. Closing this gap needs a small set of labelled real frames for fine-tuning, which the Franka phase produces anyway.

Simulation choices and known gaps

Membrane model
61-vertex triangulated disc (47 mm), MuJoCo 2D flex with bending elasticity (E = 20 MPa, 0.12 mm, 0.4 g). Edge constraints were stiffened after the defaults let the sheet stretch 25–60% when lifted. No wetting, adhesion or air.
Grasp
The tips close on the edge and a five-vertex edge patch is clamped to the hand. Clamping only three edge vertices behaves like a hinge and the sheet folds.
Contact surfaces
MuJoCo caps flex contacts at 50 per body pair, which left 11 of 61 vertices unsupported, so frit, agar and magazine tops are each built from nine separate tiles.
Rigid handling
Plates, lid, funnel, vessel and cap are carried kinematically once gripped. The 90 mm Petri dish is wider than the Franka Hand's 80 mm stroke, so dishes and lids are pinched at the rim: the real cell needs a rim gripper or tool change.
Dosing and filtration
Pouring and filtration are shown as animated liquid levels; dosing by pipette, vacuum and rinsing are not simulated. Filtration time in the model is 3 minutes (10 minutes for the slow case).
Arm
Gravity-compensated position control with differential IK (mink). A real Franka compensates gravity internally; without it the sim arm sagged 3–7 mm.

Next

Proposed Franka test (about one week once access and materials exist)

  1. Mount an overhead and a wrist camera in the same layout as the sim, and calibrate them.
  2. Collect 50–100 teleoperated demonstrations of the membrane transfer with real membranes and agar plates.
  3. Retrain the same ACT policy on real data, optionally co-trained with the sim demonstrations.
  4. Run 50 rollouts scored with the same metric definitions, judged by the perception stack.

Needed from the lab side: Panda or FR3, gripper type (and any suction), cameras and mounting points, the consumables at the arm (funnels, frit base, membranes, agar plates), network access for two camera streams, and who is on site for resets and safety. Policy inference can run remotely with roughly 100 ms latency if it sends 10–20-step action chunks; the real-time control loop stays on the arm's PC.
